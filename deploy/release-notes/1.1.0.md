These notes are written for an operator who runs their own hub and
runners and whom nobody in this project can log into (invariant 9,
D161). **Nothing in this project deploys anything.** This release makes
a build available; you upgrade when you choose, on your own machines, at
your own moment.

**Every sentence below is something the code does, and every figure says
where it came from.** Where a figure was measured on the first
consumer's own machines and reached us as a report, it is marked as
theirs: nobody here read their hosts. Where nothing was measured, that
is said instead of estimated. One hazard in this release is **reasoned
from the code and not measured**, and it is labelled that way in the
section that carries it.

## Read this first, in this order

1. **Upgrade every runner, then the hub. This is required, not
   advisory.** Why, below.
2. **There is no migration.** All three version numbers are unchanged
   from v1.0.0. Nothing in your state root has to move, and nothing in
   it is at risk. The four steps in `docs/operating.md` are the
   v0.4.1 → v1.0.0 procedure and **do not apply to this upgrade**.
3. **A job's memory ceiling can now be tighter than your
   `--job-memory-max`, and for most declarations it will be.** If you
   run a job that was surviving near the cap, read *What can bite on
   upgrade* before you upgrade, not after.

### The three numbers, unchanged

| Number | What it governs | v1.0.0 | v1.1.0 | Declared at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `PROTOCOL_VERSION` | whether two builds can talk | 3 | **3** | `herd-proto/src/lib.rs:161` |
| `RECORD_VERSION` | the shape of every `job.json` in the root | 2 | **2** | `herd-hub/src/job.rs:104` |
| `LAYOUT_VERSION` | the shape of the state root itself | 2 | **2** | `herd-hub/src/store.rs:99` |

Read out of the source this build was compiled from, at both tags. None
of the three moved, so a v1.0.0 state root is a v1.1.0 state root, and
**rolling back from v1.1.0 to v1.0.0 is safe on all three counts.**
v1.0.0 is the only release that has ever moved any of them — every
release up to v0.4.1 sits at 2, 1 and 1 — so v1.1.0 is the first
release on the far side of that move, and the first upgrade since it
that asks nothing of your state root.

Two fields were added to the protocol in this release and the number
stayed at 3 deliberately: both are `#[serde(default)]` fields on
existing frames, which is one of the three shapes the constant's own
documentation blesses as additive. An older peer that has never heard
of either field decodes the frame and reads the default. That is what
makes the fleet upgradeable one machine at a time — and also what makes
the **order** matter.

## Why every runner comes before the hub

**The self-collision fix is inert until a runner sends the new field.**
A runner that is already executing attempt 1 of a job and is offered
attempt 2 of the same job now refuses and says *which* attempt is in
the way, in a field named `still_running_attempt`. The hub uses that to
exclude the one `(job, runner)` pair and make the job **wait for the
machine it is already on** instead of dying.

An un-upgraded runner refuses in exactly the same situation and **sends
no such field**. The hub reads the absence as what it is — the runner
did not say which refusal this was — the exclusion set stays empty, and
the fix does nothing at all. **A hub upgraded alone fixes nothing.**

There is a second reason, and it is the one that can cost you a job:
see *A mixed fleet* below.

**A v1.0.0 runner will connect to a v1.1.0 hub.** The protocol number
is equal on both sides, so the handshake passes; the build versions are
a minor apart, so the hub serves the connection and prints one line
saying the two builds differ. Nothing refuses a half-upgraded fleet for
you. The order is yours to keep.

## What can bite on upgrade

**Before this release, every job on a runner was held to one figure:
`--job-memory-max`, built once at the runner's start.** A job's
`--memory-mib` was a placement requirement and never a ceiling. From
this release, a job that declared its needs is held to a ceiling derived
from **its own declaration**:

```
ceiling = (memory_mib + tmpfs_mib) × 1.1
```

clamped by `--job-memory-max`, floored at 16 MiB. In code:
`herd_runner::job_ceiling`, `herd-runner/src/confine.rs:247`, whose
whole arithmetic is `declared.unwrap_or(global).min(global)` then
`.max(MINIMUM_MEMORY_MAX)`. The 10 % is
`herd_proto::CEILING_MARGIN_PERCENT`, one constant on the contract at
`herd-proto/src/declaration.rs:663`.

**Read the `.min(global)` carefully, because it is the whole guarantee
under this feature.** A declaration *above* `--job-memory-max` is
clamped **down** to it, never up. No figure a requester sends can lift
the bound the machine's owner configured. That is invariant 2 holding
unchanged: the most any job on your machine may hold is still the
number you put on the command line.

**And read it carefully for the other reason too: the clamp only ever
tightens.** A job that declares 1792 MiB of memory and 2560 MiB of
tmpfs is granted `(1792 + 2560) × 1.1 = 4787` MiB — on a runner whose
`--job-memory-max` is 7 GiB, that is **2381 MiB less** than the same job
had under v1.0.0. A job that was surviving close to the old cap will be
OOM-killed on the new one unless its declaration is large enough to
earn the room it actually uses.

**This is not hypothetical, and the figures are the first consumer's own
and not ours.** Their `leash-stick-update` declares 1792 MiB and
reserves 2.5 GiB of tmpfs — so 4787 MiB is exactly the ceiling this
release would grant it. Two readings of that job reached us:

* one run peaked at **4727 MiB of anon + shmem** and passed. The 4787
  MiB ceiling clears that by **60 MiB**, on one observation.
* another run used **4.031 GiB anon and 3.930 GiB shmem** — about 8152
  MiB together — and was killed (D283). **4787 MiB does not come close
  to holding it.**

Both are their measurements, reported to us; neither was taken by
anybody in this project and neither is in this repository as data. We
are not telling you what to declare. We are telling you that **the
declaration you have today was never a ceiling, and from this release it
is one** — so derive it from a peak you have measured, on your own
hosts, before you upgrade the runner that runs it.

### Declare tmpfs, not memory alone

**The ceiling spans both figures because a tmpfs byte is charged to the
job's own cgroup.** Measured, 2026-10-31: 192 MiB written to `/dev/shm`
under a 64 MiB ceiling with `memory.swap.max = 0` left `shmem 62988288`
in the writing cgroup's own `memory.stat`, `memory.peak` at exactly the
ceiling, one `oom_kill`, and the file stopped short. The same 192 MiB
written to ext4 hit the same ceiling 170 times and was **never killed**,
because page cache is reclaimable and shmem under `swap.max = 0` is not
(D283).

So **a job that declares memory alone and then fills the tmpfs it
reserved is OOM-killed**, and nothing but the declaration can prevent
it. A tmpfs reservation is an **admission figure, not a bound**: it
tells the hub where the job fits. It does not stop the job writing, and
every byte it writes is charged to the same ceiling its heap is.

### Do not pre-apply the margin

**Declare the two raw figures. The contract adds the 10 %.** The
arithmetic above is applied by Herd, on the runner, from what you
declared. If you have a 4787 MiB figure in your hand, that figure is
*already* a ceiling with the margin inside it —
`(1792 + 2560) × 1.1 = 4787` — and declaring 4787 would ask for
`4787 × 1.1 = 5265`. The fixture that pins this is
`a_charge_is_the_declared_ceiling_clamped_and_floored`, in
`herd-proto/src/declaration.rs`.

A declaration below 16 MiB earns 16 MiB, on both sides: the runner
grants the floor and the hub charges the floor, so a job asking for one
mebibyte is not read as costing one.

## The self-collision fix bounds the defect. It does not remove it.

**What it fixes, and the fix is real.** The first consumer's
`j0000000655`, 2026-10-31, v1.0.0: a 71.28 s link silence, the hub's
watchdog wrote attempt 1 off while the machine was still running it, and
the job was offered **back to that same machine eight times in 0.51 s**
and ended `refused_as_full`, exit 125, with no verdict, for work no
machine had failed at. That half-second is closed. The job now waits for
the place it is already in.

**What is left, said plainly because the mechanism says it.** The
exclusion lives on the **connection**. A redial hands the hub a fresh,
empty exclusion set (`herd-hub/src/server.rs:2297`), and the hub offers
queued work on the hello — dispatch runs before the hub reads the
runner's first frame (`:4070`). So what you get is **one self-collision
refusal per connection, re-armed by every redial**. A job has a finite
refusal budget. **Repeated link flaps during one long attempt can still
exhaust it and end the job with no verdict.** If your link to the hub
flaps, this release makes that rarer. It does not make it impossible,
and nothing in this release claims to.

## A mixed fleet: a hazard we have not measured

**Stated as unmeasured on purpose. We did not quantify it, and nothing
below should be read as if we had.**

A runner built before 2026-10-31 announces how much memory it lends and
what floor it keeps — both figures predate v0.1.0 — so a v1.1.0 hub
admits work to it **against the sum of the ceilings it would grant**.
But a runner's own sum-based self-bound landed only on 2026-10-31, after
v1.0.0 was cut, so **a v1.0.0 runner still bounds itself by the job
count it announced**. Two different bounds over one machine.

With declared jobs smaller than that runner's cap, the hub can offer it
more work than it will take, and a refusal is the answer. A refusal
storm is how `j0000000655` died.

There is reason to believe this one is self-limiting: the first refusal
carries the machine's own count of what it is holding, and the hub then
holds the machine to the ceiling it announced. **We did not measure how
fast that re-arms**, so we are not telling you it is harmless and we are
not telling you it is not.

**What to do, and it costs nothing:** upgrade the whole fleet together,
or keep jobs that declare `--memory-mib` off an un-upgraded runner until
it moves. That is the same order as *Why every runner comes before the
hub*, for a second reason.

## What you should expect to gain, which may be nothing

**A runner bounded by the sum of what it granted can hold more small
jobs than a seat count let it.** Whether *your* jobs are among them is
arithmetic you can do before you upgrade, and we would rather you did it
than took a multiplier from us. There is no multiplier.

A runner's usable memory is `lent − floor`. It admits jobs while the sum
of the ceilings it has granted stays inside that. So:

```
usable  = lent − floor
ceiling = (memory_mib + tmpfs_mib) × 1.1     per job, clamped by the cap
jobs    = the largest n with n × ceiling ≤ usable
```

**On the first consumer's own shape the gain for their main job is
zero.** Their figures, read from their configuration by an agent and
relayed, not measured by us: 20 GiB lent, 6 GiB floor, so **14336 MiB**
usable; their declaration earns a **4787** MiB ceiling. Two fit
(`2 × 4787 = 9574`). Three do not: `3 × 4787 = 14361`, which overshoots
by **25 MiB**. That is a miss at any margin, the 10 % included and zero
included — the margin is not what costs them the third job; the
declaration is.

The gain appears for declarations smaller than that. It appears for
nobody by magic. Do the three lines above with your own two figures.

## What this release carries

Eight changes, each verified against the source this build was compiled
from. The decision entries named are in `docs/decisions.md` in the
repository.

1. **Per-job memory ceilings, by declaration** — `job_ceiling`,
   `herd-runner/src/confine.rs:247`, with the 10 % margin as one
   constant on the contract. Above.
2. **A runner bounds itself by the sum of the ceilings it granted**,
   replacing the seat count on any host that says how much memory it
   lends. A machine too small to hold one job at its own ceiling holds
   one anyway. The count is kept as the bound for a host that will not
   state its size, as the figure the hub is told, and as the arithmetic
   every refusal quotes (D285).
3. **The hub charges the same figure over the same set**, so every set
   the hub admits is a set the runner accepts and no attempt is spent on
   a refusal. A job with no declared figure is charged a whole
   `(lent − floor) ÷ jobs_at_once`, because that is what the runner will
   grant it — and the hub cannot tell a figureless requester's job from
   a reading it minted itself, so it charges both the same (D285, D287).
4. **An out-of-memory kill is charged to the step it happened in, and
   named.** One kill used to be reported against two steps: the counter
   the runner read counts *processes*, and with `memory.oom.group = 1`
   the runner is woken by the first of a burst of deaths, so its reading
   was partial and the rest landed in the next step's delta. The verdict
   is now read from `oom_group_kill`, which counts **events**, once,
   before the first task is signalled (D284).
5. **A requester sees what its job took.** The peak was always measured
   and always in the record; `herdctl` never printed it. It now says the
   peak with its unit, and which cap stopped the job at what figure —
   **naming that figure as the runner's own ceiling and not as your
   declaration**, which is the exact confusion the first consumer hit
   on 2026-10-30. `rc 124` stays one code for all four caps, with the
   reason in words instead (D282).
6. **The hub asks for a collection the moment the verdict lands.** It
   used to ask once per connection, at join — which races a verdict a
   runner held across a link break: the scan reads a record with no
   manifest, finds nothing owed, and the manifest is written
   milliseconds later. Nobody ever asked again. The first consumer lost
   **17 files, 43 011 442 bytes** that way on 2026-10-31, under a record
   reading `finished, exited 0` with 0 of 17 files collected, while
   their runner held every byte; a restart brought the whole collection
   home with digests matching their own copy. The question is now asked
   where it first has an answer (D291).
7. **A short collection says so on the record.** When the hub gives up
   on a streamed collection it writes one `CollectionShort` event on the
   job's own history — files here of files owed, bytes here of bytes
   owed, the runner, and the sentence the requester was given. Before
   this, the loss was *derivable* from a manifest against an emptier
   `files/` and **nothing derived it**: two lines on the hub's stdout
   and two notes to a client, neither of them a record. A nightly
   reading the record saw a green job (D292).
8. **A job is not offered back to the machine already running it**
   (D294). Above, with what it does not fix.

## What this release does not carry

**The hub's record retention.** v1.0.0's notes said it was "cut from
v1.0.0, following in v1.1". **It is not in v1.1.0.** The class map was
decided — 90 days for the merger class, 14 for every other — and the
engine, the guard, the index update and the removal order are not
built. What ships is what v1.0.0 shipped: `--output-budget`,
`--volume-free-floor`, and `herd-hub retention-preview`, which answers
what a pass *would* take and **removes nothing**. There is still no
`--apply`. **Nothing in this release removes a record, on a timer or
otherwise.** We are naming this rather than letting the version number
answer for it.

**Nothing else that v1.0.0 named as unmet has changed.** E-49 is still
waived and still unmeasured; E-50 and E-14 are still withdrawn, with
their reasons in v1.0.0's notes. Read those notes beside these: this
release does not restate them.

**One known-unstable test still ships, and is still named.**
`a_runner_killed_mid_job_gives_its_slot_back`, in
`herd-hub/tests/slots.rs`. It is a test of our own, not of your fleet,
and the promise it sits under — a slot comes back in about 60 s — is
unchanged.
