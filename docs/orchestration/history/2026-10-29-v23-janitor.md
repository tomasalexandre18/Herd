### The file

`{data root}/left-behind`, one plain-text file, written whole and
`fsync`ed, outside `jobs/` — a record of what the reaper could not do may
not live where the reaper can erase it. Header plus one entry per thing,
each entry six fields on separate lines:

```
left-behind: /…/jobs/j0000000023-1
holds: 7 kB in 3 file(s), measured 2026-10-29T16:31:45Z
left-by: job j0000000023, attempt 1
first-seen: 2026-10-29T16:31:45Z
why: Permission denied
do: remove this path as the user that owns what is in it. This runner
    put back every mode it owns and was refused anyway, so it will not
    try again.
cost-of-not: 7 kB stay on this disk, and no runner will ever take them
    away.
```

The only field anything but a person parses is `left-behind:`; that is
what makes an entry's figure survive a rewrite without the tree being
walked again.

### Where entries come from, and why that is all of them

The three places a workspace reclaim can fail, and nothing else:

| site | when |
|---|---|
| `job.rs` `reclaim_or_say` (4 callers) | a job ends, **a setup failure included** |
| `lib.rs` `drain` (`EndedJob::workspace_left`) | the runner is stopped with a job still live |
| `reclaim.rs` `reclaim_a_past_life` | startup, a workspace a killed runner left |

`root.rs:298` is a fourth *appearance* of the same failure —
`open_workspace` refusing because a same-id leftover will not clear — and
it is deliberately **not** wired, because it cannot name anything the
list does not already hold: a workspace that exists at `open_workspace`
time was left either by a previous life (the reaper wrote it down) or by
a previous attempt of this life (`reclaim_or_say` wrote it down). Adding
a fourth writer would add a path, not an entry.

### The five things it had to prove, and where each is proved

Eleven new assertions, nine in `herd-runner/src/janitor.rs`, two in
`herd-runner/src/job.rs`. All plain `#[test]`, none `#[ignore]`d, no
`HERD-SKIPPED` added.

1. **A job whose setup fails leaves an entry with the path and what it
   weighs** — `job::tests::a_workspace_a_job_left_behind_reaches_the_`
   `notebook_with_its_figure`, through `reclaim_or_say`, the one function
   every ending of a job leaves by. Plus
   `janitor::tests::what_was_left_behind_is_named_with_what_it_weighs`,
   whose figure is checkable arithmetic (3 000 + 2 000 + 2 000 = 7 kB in
   3 files) and which asserts that **every** field line carrying a figure
   has a digit in it.
2. **It survives a restart** — `janitor::tests::the_list_survives_the_`
   `restart_that_is_the_whole_reason_for_it`, through the runner's **own**
   startup path twice (`Confined::prepare`, drop, `Confined::prepare`),
   not through a stand-in for it (D211's lesson). It also asserts that
   one thing is one entry however many starts meet it, and that the entry
   keeps the moment it was first seen rather than the last boot's.
3. **An entry whose thing has been cleared is removed** —
   `janitor::tests::an_entry_whose_thing_the_owner_has_cleared_comes_`
   `off_the_list`: the owner removes the directory, the next
   `reconcile` drops the entry, says so, and the header counts 0. A list
   with nothing on it reports nothing at all.
4. **Nothing on it is something the runner could have reclaimed** —
   `janitor::tests::nothing_the_runner_could_reclaim_itself_is_ever_on_`
   `the_list`. Two locks: an entry is written only where a reclaim has
   already failed, and `write_down` asks the disk once more before
   writing, so a path that is no longer there is never written down
   whatever the caller believed. A runner that left nothing behind writes
   no file at all.
5. **An unwritable file costs nothing** — `janitor::tests::a_notebook_`
   `that_cannot_be_written_costs_the_caller_nothing` and
   `job::tests::a_notebook_that_cannot_be_written_never_costs_a_job_its_`
   `record`: with a directory in the file's place, the job's record still
   reaches the session, `write_down` and `reconcile` still return, and
   nothing is removed or renamed to clear the way. `write_down` returns
   `()`; there is nothing for a caller to handle.

### The spool warning, answered mechanically and not by habit

`write_down` **refuses** a path under `outputs/` or `held-results/`, a
path outside the data root, the data root itself and the list itself,
naming the refusal on stderr. The two directories are asked of the
`DataRoot` (`outputs_dir()`, `held_results_dir()`) and not spelled again,
so one of them moving cannot leave the guard behind. Proved by
`janitor::tests::a_spool_and_a_held_result_can_never_be_named_as_garbage`,
which also asserts the spool's bytes are still there afterwards.

And a source-level guard, `janitor::tests::this_module_takes_nothing_`
`away`: the module's code above its tests contains none of
`remove_file`, `remove_dir`, `remove_dir_all`, `rename(`,
`set_permissions`, `symlink(`, `cgroup.kill`. The list cannot destroy a
spool because the module that writes it cannot destroy anything.

### Bounded at both ends

Two paths that may not stall: a job ending and a start. The measurement
stops after 50 000 directory entries, the list stops at 1 000 entries,
and `reconcile` is one `lstat` per entry and no walk. A figure from a
walk that stopped early, or that was refused a path, is printed as **"at
least N"** with the reason beside it, never as a total and never as a
zero (D23). An empty tree is `0 bytes in 0 file(s)`, which is a figure
and not the absence of one. Proved by
`janitor::tests::a_figure_the_walk_could_not_finish_is_a_floor_and_says_so`
and `janitor::tests::the_list_stops_at_its_bound_and_keeps_what_it_has`.

`reaped at startup:` lines, in its own voice and never merged with them:
one is what the reaper took away, the other is what is still on the disk.
It names the file, the count, and up to five paths with their figures.

## Risks and findings

1. **D134 asks for two lists, and this is the second one.** D134's own
   words: the consumer asked either that the reaper run their declared
   `down`, or "at least publish the reaped job ids where a janitor can
   read them", and the chosen half was to write down "what it reaped …
   the job id, what was removed, what was killed, and when". This brief
   asked for the opposite half — strictly what the runner **could not**
   reclaim — and the two are disjoint by construction: a reaped workspace
   is something Herd *did* remove, and this list refuses it. So the
   leak D134 was reported about, state a job's `up` made **outside** its
   workspace (a workspace layer, files on a RAM tmpfs), still has no
   machine-readable record of *which jobs* were reaped, and a janitor
   cannot key off this file to clean up after them. **ORCH-012 is not
   fully discharged.** That half cannot be folded in here without
   breaking the brief's fourth requirement, and it needs a decision about
   its shape, because out-of-workspace state has no path Herd can name
   and so no figure — and "every line carries a figure" is this file's
   whole discipline. A proposed row is in the agent's report.
2. **A process the runner could not kill is silent.** `reclaim.rs`
   `kill_pid` returns `false` and `reclaim_a_past_life` says nothing at
   all in that case: the line is only pushed when the kill succeeded. A
   job process that outlives every runner is worse than a workspace that
   will not go, and today nothing names it anywhere. Small fix, not in
   this brief.
3. **A cgroup the runner could not remove is silent too.**
   `reclaim::remove_cgroup` returns `()` after 50 tries. Same shape as 2.
4. **The figure in an entry is dated, not live.** A tree that keeps
   growing after its entry was written — something still writing into a
   workspace nobody can remove — is reported at the weight it had when it
   was first seen. The alternative is re-walking every listed tree at
   every start, which is an unbounded step on the startup path (D134's
   own reason, invariant 2). The timestamp beside the figure is what makes
   this honest rather than wrong; if the trade turns out to be the wrong
   one, a `herdctl`-side or runner-side explicit re-measure is the fix,
   not a walk on the startup path.
5. **The list is bounded at 1 000 entries and keeps the oldest.** On a
   host that is leaking faster than its owner clears, the 1 001st thing
   is named only on stderr. The alternative — evicting the oldest — would
   stop naming the thing that has been on the disk longest, which is the
   worse failure.
6. **Nothing here reaches the hub.** The notebook is for the host's
   owner, by design (invariant 9, D161): the job's own record already
   carries the same sentence for the requester. If a fleet-wide view of
   leftovers is ever wanted, it is a new frame and a decision, not an
   addition to this file.

## Decisions needed

**The other half of D134 (finding 1): does a janitor get the list of
reaped jobs as well, and in what shape?** — *Recommendation:* yes, as a
separate task and a separate file, `{data root}/reaped`, one line per
reaped attempt (job id, attempt, what was removed, what was killed,
when), bounded and rotated, with the same "no spool, no held result"
guard; it is what the consumer actually asked for and it is what lets
their janitor run their own `down`. It must not be merged into
`left-behind`, whose discipline is "everything here is still on your
disk". *Fallback:* leave it, and tell the consumer plainly that Herd
names only what it could not clean, so their out-of-workspace state has
to be keyed off their own job log rather than off the runner's disk.
